Why Visit Strasbourg?

Because it is two countries at once. French street names above German timber framing, a Gothic cathedral built by masons who spoke Alsatian, and a covered market selling both choucroute and foie gras. Strasbourg is what a border looks like after a thousand years of arguing.

A city that changed country five times

Strasbourg became a Free City of the Holy Roman Empire in 1262 and stayed one for four centuries — which is why it built a cathedral and a university instead of a royal palace. In 1681 Louis XIV's army arrived and the citizens recognised him as their king. In 1871 the Germans took it back. In 1918 France took it again. In 1940 Germany again. In 1944 France, for good.

Each owner left something behind. Walk east from the cathedral and the half-timbered lanes stop dead; suddenly you are in the Neustadt, an imperial German quarter of enormous sandstone blocks built to prove a point. Today the city is one of the three capitals of the European Union, which is roughly the least likely outcome anyone could have predicted.

The tallest building on earth for 227 years

The cathedral's north spire reaches 142 metres. From 1647 until 1874 nothing built by human hands stood higher — not in Europe, not anywhere. It is made of pink Vosges sandstone, which is why the whole thing glows rose at sunset and looks bruised in the rain.

The south spire was never built. The masons ran out of money, and then out of certainty. The result is the most famous asymmetry in Gothic architecture: one tower reaching, one stump waiting.

Hear how they raised that spire — and why the second one never happened — standing on the square where the masons worked.

The summer everyone danced

In July 1518 a woman called Frau Troffea stepped into a Strasbourg street and began to dance. She did not stop. Within a week there were thirty-four dancers; within a month, around four hundred. The city council, advised that the cure was more dancing, cleared two guildhalls and hired musicians.

People danced until they collapsed. Contemporary accounts say some died of stroke and exhaustion. Nobody has ever fully explained it — ergot poisoning, mass hysteria, a stress cult; the arguments are still running. It happened here, in these streets, and almost no visitor knows it.

Petite France was never pretty

The postcard quarter with the flower boxes and the leaning timbers was the working end of town: tanners, millers and fishermen, using the river because their trades stank. The name is not romantic either. It comes from a sixteenth-century hospital for soldiers with syphilis — what the Germans called Franzosenkrankheit, the French disease.

The tanners' houses still show it. Those steep open lofts under the roofs were drying racks for hides. Now they hold geraniums.

Capital of Christmas since 1570

The Christkindelsmärik has run since 1570, which makes it the oldest Christmas market in France and one of the oldest in Europe. It began as a Protestant replacement for the Catholic Saint Nicholas fair — a market for the Christ Child instead of the saint. Four and a half centuries later it draws around two million visitors across four weeks and turns the entire island into one lit-up shop.

It is magnificent and it is completely overrun. Come for it deliberately, or come in October and have the same city to yourself.

What You'll Discover on the Tour

Thirty-three stops, walked in one loop from the station to the cathedral. These are six of them.

The Cathedral Facade

Hundreds of figures across a wall of pink sandstone — and a stone competition between the wise and foolish virgins that visitors walk past every day.

The Astronomical Clock

A Renaissance machine that still parades its apostles past Christ at 12:30, watched by a rooster that has crowed since the sixteenth century.

Petite France

Where the Ill splits into four channels and the tanners built their drying lofts. The prettiest working-class slum in France.

Covered Bridges & Barrage Vauban

Vauban's dam could flood the whole southern approach to drown an attacking army. Its roof terrace has the best free view in Strasbourg.

Kammerzell House

1427 at the base, 1589 above: seventy-five carved windows paid for by a cheese merchant who wanted everyone to know it.

Place Gutenberg

Johannes Gutenberg spent a decade here working out how to copy a book. He left for Mainz — and Strasbourg has been slightly annoyed about it ever since.

Read this before you book anything

You cannot buy cathedral tickets online — and that is fine

Every page promising skip-the-line entry to the astronomical clock or the tower platform is selling you something that does not exist. Both are sold on site only, in cash or by card, on the day. The nave is free. What you can book ahead are boat trips, guided walks, Alsace day trips and Colmar excursions — and that is where a booking site actually helps.

  • Clock: queue from 11:30 at the Saint-Michel entrance, €3 — get there by 11:45 in summer
  • Platform: no queue system at all, just steps. Go first thing or late afternoon
  • The €5 Strasbourg City Card takes €4 off the platform and €4 off every museum

Frequently Asked Questions

The things people actually ask before they come.

Is Strasbourg Cathedral free to visit?

The nave is free — walk in, look up, leave. You pay only for two extras: the astronomical clock show at 12:30 (€3) and the climb to the platform (€10, 330 steps). Both are sold on the spot on the day; there is no online booking and no skip-the-line ticket, whatever other sites tell you.

How many days do you need in Strasbourg?

One full day covers the island — cathedral, Petite France, the covered bridges and Place Kléber. Two days lets you add the Neustadt, a museum and a boat trip without rushing. Three days and you can take the train to Colmar or spend an afternoon on the wine route.

When is the Strasbourg Christmas market in 2026?

The city has not published the 2026 dates yet — as of late July 2026 the official site still shows the 2025 edition. It always runs from the last week of November to 24 December. We will update this page the day the dates are confirmed.

Is the audio tour available offline?

Yes. Download it once on Wi-Fi and everything — audio, map, images — works with no signal at all. Useful in Strasbourg, where the old streets are narrow and reception is patchy.

Does the app start the audio automatically at each stop?

No, and that is deliberate. The offline GPS map shows where you are, but you press play yourself when you reach the spot. Phone GPS accuracy varies too much for automatic triggering to be reliable in a city of tall stone buildings.

What languages is the audio guide in?

Eight: English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish and Russian. You choose one at purchase.

How do I get to Strasbourg from Paris?

By TGV from Gare de l'Est: 19 departures a day, fastest 1 h 45, fares from around €32 if you book ahead. Strasbourg station is a ten-minute walk from Petite France, which is exactly where our tour begins. Check times and fares on SNCF.

Is Strasbourg French or German?

French — and has been continuously since 1944. But it was German from 1871 to 1918 and again from 1940 to 1944, and part of the Holy Roman Empire for six centuries before that. The local language, Alsatian, is a Germanic dialect. The architecture, the food and the surnames all sit somewhere in between.

Can you visit the European Parliament?

Yes, free of charge. Outside plenary weeks individuals do not need to book. Bring a physical passport or national ID card — photocopies, driving licences and photos on your phone are all refused at security.

Is the Alsatian Museum open?

No. The Musée Alsacien is closed for renovation until 1 June 2028. The Historical Museum next door and the three museums inside Palais Rohan are all open, closed Mondays.
