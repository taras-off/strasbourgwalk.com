Strasbourg's Astronomical Clock: The 12:30 Show, Tickets and What It Shows

The astronomical clock in Strasbourg Cathedral has two sets of hands. The silver ones show the same time as your phone, and the gold ones show Strasbourg's mean solar time, which in winter runs about half an hour behind. The main show begins at 12:30, when the twelve apostles come out before Christ and the rooster above them crows three times.

The clock stands in the south transept, and whenever the nave is open you can look at it without a ticket. Its figures move every quarter of an hour. The full show runs once a day, and to see it you need a ticket. The audience goes in through a separate entrance. A few steps away in the same transept rises the Pillar of Angels, a 13th-century sculpted column, while the rest of Strasbourg Cathedral, with its facade, stained glass and viewing platform, is best explored separately, without one eye on 12:30.

The €4 ticket is not what you need to see the clock. It is for the 12:30 show, with the film and the procession of the apostles.

The Christmas market opens on Friday, November 27, 2026, and the city has not yet announced the closing date. While the market is on, the cathedral is open Monday to Saturday from 08:30 to 17:45 without a midday break, and visitors enter through the south portal on Place du Château. There is no clock show during those weeks.

Why 12:30

The time was not picked to suit tourists. The silver hands show official time, and the gold hands show Strasbourg's mean solar time. The city lies well west of the meridian that sets Central European Time, and in winter the gap between the two is about half an hour. So noon on the gold hands falls at 12:30 by our watches. The chimes and the astronomical dials follow the gold hands.

You have probably noticed that it gets dark early in winter and the days stretch out in summer: the sun does not keep the same schedule all year. Noon is no exception. The moment when the sun stands highest over the cathedral comes earlier in some months and later in others, sometimes by a quarter of an hour. Mean solar time smooths that out, so on it every day lasts exactly 24 hours. That makes 12:30 noon by Schwilgué's clock, not by the sun on any given day.

In summer France moves to daylight saving time, and local noon would drift to 13:30. To prevent that, the clock is adjusted for the summer, and the apostles still come out at 12:30 official time.

What happens on the clock

Four figures, the four ages of a human life, take turns passing before Death: a child at the first quarter, a youth at the half hour, a grown man at the third quarter and an old man at the top of the hour. Above them, one angel strikes each quarter while a second turns over an hourglass.

At 12:30 the figures on the upper tier start moving too. The twelve apostles pass before Christ, who blesses them. The rooster on the small tower to the left flaps its wings and crows three times, recalling Peter's three denials. Lower down, above the calendar, the chariots of the days of the week each carry their own god: Apollo on Sundays, Diana, goddess of the Moon, on Mondays, then Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn. Say the French weekdays out loud, lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, and you can hear the same names.

The figures are small, and the niches they move in sit high up. It is a short mechanical show, not a light-and-sound spectacle. Anyone expecting a performance on the scale of the cathedral may come away disappointed. The clock rewards people who know what it is calculating.

What the mechanism calculates

Beneath the figures runs a perpetual calendar. A large ring shows the fixed-date feasts and saints' days. The mechanism handles leap years by itself. On the night of January 1, a separate unit on the left, the comput ecclésiastique, works out the date of Easter for the coming year, and with it all the movable feasts. Scholars and churchmen argued for centuries over how to calculate Easter, and here gears do it once a year.

The center of the lower section shows the positions of the sun and moon, and the mechanism reproduces solar and lunar eclipses. On the planetary dial, the planets out to Saturn circle the sun. The celestial sphere shows the sky as seen from Strasbourg, and one of its wheels takes 25,806 years to complete a turn. That is how the mechanism models precession, the slow wobble of the Earth's axis, which takes about 25,800 years.

Three generations of clocks

The clock you see is the cathedral's third.

The first clock, 1354. The Clock of the Three Kings stood against the opposite wall of the transept. It stopped in the early 16th century, but its rooster survived. Made of painted wood and wrought iron, the bird was already flapping its wings and crowing in the 14th century. It is now considered the oldest surviving automaton in the West, and it stands in the Museum of Decorative Arts in the Palais Rohan, across the square from the cathedral.

The second clock, 1547–1570s. Work on it soon came to a halt, and it was finished only some twenty-five years later, on the spot where the current clock stands. It brought together the mathematician Conrad Dasypodius, the clockmakers Isaac and Josias Habrecht and the painter Tobias Stimmer, who decorated the case. That clock made the city famous, and in 1788 its iron wheels finally wore out and it stood still for half a century.

The third clock, 1838–1842. This is the one in front of you. Its mechanism was built by Jean-Baptiste Schwilgué, a self-taught Alsatian engineer who had wanted to repair the clock since boyhood. He was not allowed to build a new case: the city authorities insisted on keeping the Renaissance one. So a 16th-century cabinet now holds a 19th-century mechanism.

The legend of the blinded clockmaker

One of the city's stories about the clock goes like this: the authorities supposedly had its maker blinded so he could never build another one for a rival city. Prague tells almost exactly the same legend about its astronomical clock.

The records do not support it. Isaac Habrecht lived for nearly half a century after the Strasbourg clock and kept working. The British Museum holds a large carillon clock he made in Strasbourg in 1589, and its dials and figures copy the cathedral's in miniature. If anything, the legend shows how highly the city valued its clock.

How to get into the show

  1. Buy your ticket on the day. From 10:00 to 11:00 tickets are sold at the cathedral shops, and from 11:30 at the show entrance. The cathedral does not sell them in advance, online or anywhere else.
  2. Find the separate entrance. It is not the main door on the cathedral square: walk around the right-hand side of the cathedral to Place du Château, and from there through to Place Saint-Michel.
  3. Arrive by 11:30. The figures are set high and many details are small, so it pays to get a place close to the clock. From 12:00 a film about the astronomical clock plays on a screen nearby, so by 12:30 you will know what to watch for.
  4. Be wary of skip-the-line offers. If someone online offers you priority entry to the clock, it is most likely a guided tour. Walks like that are among the Strasbourg Cathedral tours on GetYourGuide, but they usually don't include a ticket for the show.
  5. Keep exploring afterward. From 12:45 the nave usually reopens to everyone, and unless a service is on, you can stay and see the rest of the cathedral.

Frequently asked questions

What time is the Strasbourg astronomical clock show?

Once a day at 12:30, with a film from 12:00 before it. There is no show on Sundays, on church holidays with an 11:00 mass or during the Christmas market.

How much is a ticket?

€4, with reduced rates for children, students and groups. Free under 6.

Can I buy tickets in advance?

No. The cathedral sells tickets only on the day: 10:00–11:00 at the cathedral shops and from 11:30 at the entrance.

Why is the show at 12:30 and not at noon?

The clock runs on Strasbourg's mean solar time, which in winter is about half an hour behind official time. Noon on its gold hands falls at 12:30. In summer the clock is adjusted so the show stays at 12:30.

Can I see the clock for free?

Yes, whenever the nave is open. The figures of the four ages move every quarter hour. You only need a ticket for the 12:30 show.

Who built the astronomical clock?

The current mechanism was built by Jean-Baptiste Schwilgué between 1838 and 1842. It sits in the Renaissance case of the second clock, made in the 1570s by Conrad Dasypodius, the Habrecht brothers and Tobias Stimmer.
